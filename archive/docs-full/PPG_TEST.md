# Heart-rate sensor test

The current iPhone export contains one snapshot per minute, so it can miss short valid and invalid periods. To capture a useful follow-up:

For a quick check, keep Serial Monitor at 115200 baud with all recording modes set to `0`, send `i` to enable the diagnostic status line, and paste several status lines. `contact=1` confirms optical level only; `rel` is IR pulse RMS divided by its DC baseline, `ibi` is a diagnostic count of accepted beat intervals, and `hr_valid=1` is required before `hr` is current. `fifo_ovf`, `gaps`, `raw_bad`, and `motion_reject` are cumulative counters; compare successive lines for new failures.

`weak` counts episodes where pulse amplitude falls below the gate, `early` counts peaks rejected inside the refractory interval, `long` counts intervals outside the allowed range that restarted beat acquisition, and `unstable` counts five-beat windows with inconsistent timing. These counters distinguish a weak optical signal from a peak-detection failure.

`period` is the best normalized IR waveform correlation over 0.33–1.50 seconds; `lag` is the selected period in 10 ms samples, and `dc_drift` is the two-second fractional change in the IR baseline. The estimator requires correlation at least 0.65, matching red and IR waveforms, low baseline drift, and two consistent scans before publishing HR. `ibi` can be zero while HR is valid because peak timing is diagnostic only.

`led` is the red and IR pulse-current register value. Normal is `36` (`0x24`, about 7.2 mA per LED); wrist is `44` (`0x2C`, about 8.8 mA); moderate is `56` (`0x38`, about 11.2 mA); high is `100` (`0x64`, about 20 mA). Wrist is the boot and capture default. Send `N`, `W`, `M`, or `H` in Serial Monitor, or `L` followed by a value from 1 to 100 and a newline; pass `normal`, `wrist`, `moderate`, `high`, or the number as the optional final argument to `replay/capture_ppg.py`. Switching levels clears the HR history and FIFO.

A 30-second wrist sweep at one placement gave these replay results (unclipped unless noted): high clipped every sample; `60` and `52` produced no valid HR; `56` produced valid HR for about 15% of a 20-second trace; `48` about 11%; `44` about 78%, ranging 87–94 bpm; normal (`36`) produced none. These settings have no reference pulse measurement and are not accuracy results; placement and skin differences may move the best setting. The MAX30102 has no green LED.

The estimator accepts only a local maximum of the waveform correlation between 0.34 s and 1.49 s, and never a period shorter than 0.40 s (150 bpm). Slow wrist-pressure or breathing drift correlates highly at every short lag without forming a peak; it previously surfaced as 182 bpm at the 0.33 s search limit. A status line with `lag=0` or `hr_valid=0` while `period` is high is that rejection, not a missed beat. A 60-second wrist trace at `44` with IR DC near 137,000 counts contained no cardiac line in its spectrum and was correctly rejected, while the earlier valid trace at the same setting had IR DC near 166,000; placement, not LED current, made the difference. A second upward sweep (`48`, `56`, `64`, `72`, `80`, 20 s each, hand-held on the wrist) confirmed this: `80` clipped every sample, `72` reached 260,000 counts, and none produced a sustained estimate. In that sweep the IR baseline moved 3–10% per two seconds (up to 50%), against 0.3% in the valid trace; a wrist pulse is about 0.1% of baseline, so contact movement of that size buries it at any LED current. Strap or tape the sensor to the wrist and rest the forearm before judging a setting.

Strapped-wrist processing changes, from a 60 s strapped trace at `44` (IR baseline about 157,000): the pulse appeared as a 1.4–1.5 Hz line but 0.5–0.7 Hz pressure and breathing drift was 3–10x stronger. The firmware now applies two further first-order high-pass stages (about 0.7 Hz each) on the AC path before correlation; on that trace usable scans rose from 9 to 30 of 57 and the no-pulse trace stayed at zero. Residual 0.7 Hz drift still produced 2–3 scan bursts at roughly twice the beat lag (130–145 samples), so a candidate more than 25% away from a rate published in the last 15 s must persist for four scans, and any period over 1.0 s needs three. The pulse-amplitude floors are now 30 counts RMS and 0.00015 of baseline (uncovered noise is about 11 counts; valid wrist pulses were 57–115 counts), and the weak gate withholds HR without discarding the 3.5 s waveform history. Replay of all recorded traces after these changes: finger 50% valid at 74–82 bpm, strapped wrist 32% at 81–87 bpm, earlier wrist 78% at 85–91 bpm, no-pulse trace 0%, and no half-rate values anywhere. Live on the strapped wrist the board published 81–82 bpm intermittently at quality 3. None of this has a reference pulse measurement yet. In use, the sensor read more consistently strapped to the outside (back) of the wrist than to the inside; keep that placement for further wrist tests and treat the enclosure design accordingly.

When moving the sensor between a finger and wrist, note `reposition`: it counts large optical baseline changes and resets pulse tracking. A new heart-rate estimate needs several clean beats after the move. If it remains unavailable on the finger, capture status lines for at least 30 seconds after returning, including `contact`, `ir_dc`, `ir_rms`, `rel`, `ibi`, `q`, and `reposition`.

1. Rebuild the iPhone app and turn on **Detailed recording** in the Device tab. This saves each half-second BLE snapshot; export its CSV after the test.
2. For raw optical samples, set `RECORD_PPG` to `1` and leave the other Serial modes at `0` in `HealthWatch/HealthWatch.ino`. Upload the firmware, set Serial to 921600 baud, and save the CSV stream. The header starts with `timestamp_us,red,ir`.
   On macOS, the normal firmware can also stream a minimal raw trace on command. Close Arduino Serial Monitor, then run `python3 replay/capture_ppg.py /dev/cu.usbserial-0001 60 ~/Downloads/ppg.csv` from the project directory. The script sends `p` to start, records red/IR samples for 60 seconds, and sends `s` to stop. It reports malformed startup rows and timing gaps; a successful 60-second run should contain roughly 6000 samples. Use the actual port name if it differs.
3. For the current bench test, hold a fingertip lightly and consistently over the optical window and shield it from room light. Record two minutes of quiet rest, one minute of deliberate sensor or finger movement, then another two minutes of quiet rest. Note the start and end times of each segment. Wrist measurements require a separate test after the sensor is mounted in an enclosure.
4. If available, record a simultaneous independent pulse reference and its timestamps. A manual pulse count over a timed interval is useful for a rough check, but cannot validate every beat.
5. Send the raw PPG CSV, the app CSV, and the segment notes together. The raw file contains the sample timing, signal amplitude, contact quality, beat count, motion rejection, FIFO overflow, and I2C error counters needed to distinguish acquisition failures from algorithm rejection.

`quality` codes in the raw CSV: `0` no contact, `1` invalid or settling, `2` motion degraded, `3` good. `heart_rate_bpm` is only a current estimate when `hr_valid=1`; otherwise it may retain an old numeric value and must not be interpreted as current. A plausible number by itself is not evidence of accuracy.

Serial capture can perturb timing. Check `timing_gaps`, `fifo_overflows`, and `i2c_errors`; if they increase rapidly during capture, repeat with a shorter segment or a faster Serial transport. The firmware's signal thresholds are provisional until representative recordings are evaluated.
